01

What's at stake

Infrastructure failure gets paid for twice — once in currency, once in response time.

A power utility sheds the wrong feeder group and a hospital generator burns fuel it didn't need to. Every feeder group switched off ripples downstream: traffic lights die at intersections, telecom towers run down finite battery reserves, generators burn fuel on the clock. None of that shows up in the shedding schedule itself — it shows up later, in an ambulance stuck at a dead intersection. We model that ripple as part of the optimization, not as an afterthought.

02

The graph problem

Why this is hard, precisely.

Any network of shared, constrained infrastructure reduces to the same shape: a graph. Feeder groups, vehicles, berths, crews, pump stations — whatever the domain, they become nodes. The rules that couple them — shared substations, overlapping shifts, blocked road segments — become edges. Scheduling or allocating across that graph, subject to those edges, is what actually has to be solved. Everything else is implementation detail.

Nodes
The discrete units being scheduled or routed.
Edges
The constraints and dependencies between them.
Objective
The cost function being minimized.
Constraints
The rules that can never be violated — encoded as penalties, not preferences.

The reason this can't be solved by inspection is scale. Eskom alone runs load-shedding across dozens of feeder groups, staged up to eight deep. Treat each feeder group's state in each stage window as one binary decision, and a mid-sized city's shedding schedule alone produces more possible combinations than there are atoms in the observable universe.

40 feeder groups × 8 stage windows   = 320 binary variables
2 ^ 320 possible schedules           ≈ 2.1 × 10^96
atoms in the observable universe     ≈ 1.0 × 10^80

No one is checking all of those by hand, in a spreadsheet, or with a greedy solver that commits early and never looks back. Classical heuristics — greedy assignment, simulated annealing — return a schedule, not the schedule. They approximate, and the gap between their answer and the true optimum is exactly the cost and the response-time delay that shows up later, downstream, on someone else's budget.

It gets harder before it gets more tractable: the objective itself is coupled. What you should shed depends on what it will cost downstream — whose response is delayed, whose pressure zone fails — and that downstream cost depends on what you shed. Solving one layer well requires a signal from the other. That's a bi-level optimization, not a flat schedule, and it's the shape of nearly every infrastructure-failure problem we've been asked to look at.

minimize xᵀQx x ∈ {0,1}ⁿ

We cast the objective as a QUBO — a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization — the standard form combinatorial problems take before a quantum solver can touch them. Every hard constraint becomes a penalty term inside Q; every soft preference becomes a weight. The graph goes in, the schedule comes out.

This is specifically the class of problem where near-term quantum hardware has a credible, testable edge over classical heuristics — not because quantum computers are faster at everything, but because QAOA and VQE explore superpositions across the objective's structure instead of committing to one branch at a time. We benchmark every solve against the best classical heuristic on the identical instance, so the edge is something you can see, not something we ask you to take on faith.

Proof of concept

Grid-Aware Emergency Resource Allocation

This is the case study we're solving first, in the open, before we take the model to any other sector. South Africa's Eskom sheds power in stages — each stage removing another 1,000MW of national demand, in rotating blocks that stretch from two hours to four as the stages climb, up to eight stages and 8,000MW deep.1 Choosing which feeder groups go dark, and when, is already a large constrained combinatorial problem. But the schedule doesn't stop at the substation: it degrades a second network downstream — the one ambulances have to route through, on travel times and reliability the grid layer just changed.

Layer 1 — the grid

Feeder groups as nodes, shared-infrastructure and blackout-window constraints as edges. Formulated as a QUBO, solved with QAOA / VQE, benchmarked against simulated annealing.

Layer 2 — the response network

Intersections, depots, and hospitals as nodes; road segments as edges, with time-varying weights driven by Layer 1's schedule — dead traffic lights, draining backup power.

H = A · Σₜ (Σᵢ loadᵢ · xᵢᵗ − target_shedₜ)²     hit the MW shed target per stage
  + B · Σ_{i ∈ critical} xᵢᵗ                     never shed hospitals / water / telecom
  + C · Σ_{(i,j) ∈ E₁} xᵢᵗ · xⱼᵗ                  no two coupled groups dark at once
  + D · Varₜ(Σₜ xᵢᵗ)                              fair rotation across groups
  + E · Σᵢ riskᵢ · xᵢᵗ                            signal fed back from Layer 2
Classical risk model → Quantum QUBO solve → Shed schedule → Road network weights → Classical routing

Solving Layer 1 well requires a signal from Layer 2, and Layer 2's inputs are entirely shaped by Layer 1's output — a genuine bi-level loop, not two models bolted together after the fact. It's the same shape of problem we look for in every engagement: two systems that only look separable until someone has to schedule both of them at once.
